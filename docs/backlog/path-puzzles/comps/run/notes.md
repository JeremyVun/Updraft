## lane.png
The girl sits on an ordinary door as a restrained gust ripple carries it through a pinch between drowned roof ends, while the kitten runs above and the fog fills the far end. Two broken roof pairs and side openings preserve a glimpse of the open room, but the image also exposes the cost: making this water genuinely too narrow for the boat concentrates the architecture around her. If prototyped, prove the door's footprint, the boat's clearance and both embark/disembark contacts with actual models before decorating the lane.

## church.png
The low view places her on the nave ridge below the kitten's belfry perch, with ivy explaining its climb; across the water the lighthouse beam and the caught boat's red sail and lantern remain legible through the fog. Make the tree physically arrest the boat's bow, then let the player's gust peel it away into a short unobstructed approach to the nave. Preserve the pause before the storm: the roof, small faces, warm lantern and lighthouse beam should still read as the ambient colour cools.

## Does the middle piece earn its place?
**My recommendation is to let her own way carry this stretch, rather than build the 40 m door ride.** The low, vulnerable door image is appealing and its rhyme with the wash-tub is clear, but forty metres of sitting interrupts the newly established foot-run spine and repeats a gusted floating-object interaction between the tree and swing. Its stronger idea is the contrast of height—the cat above, the girl close to the water—not a long lane.

If a pacing test shows that the room needs a breath, retain only a short door crossing through one existing pinch, reached and left by her own movement, then immediately return to coping and ridge traversal. Otherwise spend this stretch on a slightly descending lean-to, a dogleg of garden wall, one modest hop and a higher roof that restores the view of church and lighthouse; keep that movement continuous and unpuzzled. Removing the door does not mean replacing it with forty metres of straight wall.

## What would break the room
- **Continuous rows to justify the lane.** A 40 m canal with two unbroken banks would change this drowned village into a street; preserve sideways views of water and accept dropping the lane if the existing footprints do not make a convincing pinch.
- **A causeway under the gold line.** The line is route intent, not permission for a continuous boardwalk or a raised masonry bridge. Use only local garden boundaries with corners, breaks and believable former plots; add isolated roofs sparingly where measured reach requires them.
- **A planted island or plaza for the green.** Its ground stays drowned; a bare swing tree, a few boundary remnants and the absence of houses are enough to name the old green.
- **A dense church precinct.** Give the nave only a short submerged churchyard-wall fragment and generous water around it, with the lighthouse visibly across water. Do not translate the plan's small roof details into continuous quays, ornamental fences or greenery around every house.
- **Accidentally retaining the old spire.** The original church location becomes ordinary roofs; the new tower belongs about 120 m farther north. Keep the church/lighthouse pair readable from the run without making them huge or pulling them forward to fill the frame.
- **Treating the painted plan as collision data.** Preserve the generated village's source positions, measure every coping/hop and the total route in game units, and test fog timing against actual traversal time; the map's fog contours are dramatic timing marks, not fixed hard walls. Any gold segment drawn over a gap still needs a buildable contact solution, and none of these paintings proves the proposed 165 m route is already traversable.
